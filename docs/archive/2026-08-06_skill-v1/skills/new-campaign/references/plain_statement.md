# The Plain Statement — always the first deliverable

Before any name. Before any tagline. Before any collection or rail is proposed.

**No campaign concept, name, or line may be presented until the Plain Statement has been shown
and the human has reacted to it.** If you are reaching for a name and this does not exist yet,
you are working in the wrong order.

---

## Why it comes first

Naming before the concept is stated is what produces slop. A name generated cold — optimised for
punch, in isolation — pattern-matches to "clever tagline" without carrying an idea. The
statement forces the thinking to be right and legible before anything has to sound good.

It is also the artefact the human can argue with. A tagline invites "I don't like it." A plain
statement invites "that part isn't true" — which is the feedback that actually improves the work.

---

## Format

**Outline, bullet-driven. Not flowing paragraphs.** Scannable — the human should be able to
disagree with a single line without unpicking a paragraph.

Plain language throughout. No campaign voice yet. If a bullet sounds like copy, rewrite it as a
fact.

---

## Required sections

### 1 · What it is
- Who it is for, what window it runs in, what territory it covers
- Two to four bullets. Flat and factual. This is the elevator description, not the pitch
- **Never "sells".** Shopya is discovery, not retail — a campaign is a lens on what is
  happening, not an offer. If a bullet describes merchandise being moved, rewrite it

### 2 · The moment — and why it is real
- The cultural or seasonal condition the campaign sits inside
- What is actually true about this time of year for this audience
- Why nobody merchandises into it well, if that is the case
- **Every bullet must be defensible.** If you would argue with it, cut it

### 3 · What the data says
- The recon findings, each with the evidence attached
- Search-behaviour patterns, territories with real demand, saturation and gaps
- Explicitly mark anything that is inference rather than measurement
- **Never present a keyword finding as the campaign idea** — it is evidence a tension is real

### 4 · Why this is ours and not a generic seasonal push
- The structural reason this campaign belongs to THIS product and not a competitor
- Test: could a rival run this unchanged? If yes, the section has failed and the concept is generic

### 5 · What the world of it looks like
- The territories it touches, in plain nouns. What someone would be discovering
- The editorial approach — what we would be saying, what the browse feels like
- What belongs to other seams (editorial guides, hero, skin) rather than to us
- **No names. No counts. No collections, rails, tags, verticals or slots.** Structure is a
  later stage and stating it here is how concept work collapses into merchandising

### 6 · Open questions
- What is still unresolved, what needs verifying, what you had to assume

---

## The rule at the end

Close with an explicit statement that **no name has been chosen yet**, and ask whether the
thinking is right before proceeding to naming.

---

## Naming conventions — apply only AFTER the statement is agreed

1. **Separate the name from the copy.** A seasonal campaign needs an editorial NAME — a noun, a
   place, a moment ("The Turn", "Pre-Fall", "The August Issue"). Names are not statements. Making
   the name do aphorism work is the error.
2. **Ban the two-part pivot.** "X does this. Y does that." A full stop in the middle with a
   reversal after it is the tell. Delete it.
3. **Write the paragraph first, find the name inside it.** Never propose a name that did not come
   out of prose that already existed.
4. **Every clause must be literally true.** "The calendar turned. The weather didn't" failed this
   — when seasons change the weather changes; that is the mechanism. A line that sounds like an
   insight but states a falsehood is the worst failure mode.
5. **Say it aloud.** If no person would say it in a sentence, it is copy-shaped, not copy.

### Calibration

**"Still too hot for fall. Already bored of summer."** — the client's own line, and the standing
quality bar.

It works because both clauses are literally true, it names something slightly unflattering
(boredom) which is why it reads honest, it is conversational rather than aphoristic, and it is
specific. Measure candidates against it. If a candidate is worse, do not present it.
